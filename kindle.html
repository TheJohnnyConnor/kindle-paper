Why it matters: A ballpark number is never heard as a ballpark — the customer keeps it like a quote, and the contractor eats the gap between the guess and the real cost. Change orders — “trust me” should sign it — “The construction customer who says ‘trust me’ should have no problem signing what they just promised.” — @montesinolaw. Why it matters: The refusal to sign is the tell — if the promise is real, putting it in writing costs nothing. Verbal agreements keep costing contractors money because the other side remembers the conversation differently. Scheduling — a compressed schedule, uncompressed costs — “If a construction schedule gets compressed, costs usually do not compress with it.” — @montesinolaw. Why it matters: The client wants the same job in less time and expects the price to shrink with the calendar. It doesn’t — overtime, rush materials and stacked crews cost more, and the contractor absorbs it unless the price is re-set. Canada · Defence · A3

Pentagon rebukes Canada over defence spending and F-35 review

The U.S. Defence Department released a sharply worded fact sheet on Monday criticizing Canada for failing to meet its military commitments, calling its defence shortfalls self-evident and questioning its reliability as a partner in defending North America. It cited only 41.9% of Canada’s air force fleet and 53.9% of its naval fleet serviceable in 2025–26, with army readiness at 48%, and warned that reviewing the planned purchase of 88 American-made F-35s could worsen personnel shortages and create uncertainty for NORAD. Canada has met the old NATO 2% target and pledged 5% combined defence/security spending by 2035; its latest budget forecasts 4.1% of GDP by 2027–28, with core military spending at 2.6%.
